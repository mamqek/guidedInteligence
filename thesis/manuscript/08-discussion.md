# Discussion

The evaluation distinguishes three increasingly strong claims about repository retrieval. File-level overlap establishes that a system locates a file associated with the verified resolution. Required-evidence completeness establishes that the exact pre-resolution source units defined for a testcase remain jointly available after final selection. Neither result establishes that a response model correctly interprets the relationships among those units. This distinction governs the interpretation below: the study evaluates localisation and source availability, while understanding remains a claim that would require response-level assessment.

Structural evidence occupies an intermediate position. Resolving a source range to its enclosing owner makes identity and context more precise, and a verified graph relationship can direct inspection towards another participant. However, a graph edge does not establish that its endpoint answers the question, just as selecting both endpoints does not show that a model understands their relationship. The systems' own `coverage_status` and `sufficient` outputs are similarly treated as judgements produced by the evaluated system rather than external truth. With these boundaries established, the three research questions can be answered without equating a successful file hit with a complete repository explanation.

## RQ1: Constructing Auditable Repository Evidence

The first research question asks how retrieved source can become an evidence set whose support and selection history can be inspected. Guided Intelligence answers this through explicit evidence identity and an application-controlled lifecycle. A source observation retains its repository location and discovery history; structural resolution can add an owner identity without replacing source that cannot be resolved reliably. Semantic qualification separates material that supports a claim from leads that may guide later navigation. Deferred and dormant alternatives remain distinguishable from rejected evidence, and final evidence selection is represented as a separate decision rather than an implicit consequence of retrieval.

This design provides more than a final citation. Grounded-answer research shows why a source reference should support the claim attached to it [@Gao2023ALCE], while provenance models emphasise the entities and activities involved in producing an artefact [@W3C2013PROV]. The implemented lifecycle applies the same principle inside retrieval. It records how source enters the pipeline, which bounded decisions act on it, and whether it reaches final evidence. The contribution is therefore not that provenance makes a semantic judgement correct. It is that the judgement, its visible source, and its effect on later evidence can be inspected separately.

The corpus-wide audit demonstrates the practical value of this separation. Missing required source does not arise only when the initial search fails. In Full Workspace, required units first become unavailable at raw retrieval, initial comparison, qualification or recovery, candidate-pool construction, and final selection. A final miss consequently cannot be diagnosed from the final file list alone. Vue 10803 gives the clearest example: the responsible file remains selected in every Full Workspace repetition, but a required helper reaches the candidate pool and is omitted by final selection in three. File-level success therefore conceals a later source-level loss that the lifecycle trace makes visible.

The strength of this tracing claim must remain precise. Final-selection omissions are directly recorded. Earlier boundaries are inferred by comparing the last recorded stage where a unit is visible with the next stage where it is unavailable. They locate the boundary at which availability changes, but they do not necessarily identify the exact internal operation or semantic judgement responsible. The current evidence objects also retain summary provenance rather than one fully materialised end-to-end lineage. RQ1 is therefore answered as a demonstrated capacity to inspect evidence construction and localise losses, not as proof that every decision is correct or every item has a complete self-contained history.

## RQ2: Contributions of Structure and Adaptive Exploration

The second research question concerns CodeGraph and bounded adaptive exploration within Workspace. The results show that adaptive exploration provides a modest quality improvement at a substantial model-token cost. Enabling the controller improves P@1, R@5, and NDCG@5 under both structural settings, and required-unit survival is higher in Full Workspace than in the controller-disabled condition. At the same time, the controller accounts for most of the additional native retrieval-flow tokens. Later exploration can therefore recover and reprioritise useful source, but the measured benefit is not large enough to describe it as a general solution to incomplete evidence.

CodeGraph has a different contribution. Its aggregate file-ranking effect is small and measure-dependent: it slightly improves recall while slightly weakening the earliest-rank precision and NDCG when the controller is enabled. This explains why graphless Workspace remains competitive. Hybrid lexical and semantic retrieval already localises many issue-relevant files, and adding structurally related candidates can create competition without guaranteeing greater semantic relevance. This result accords with the distinction in prior work between structural reachability and evidence: dependency and graph navigation can recover poorly ranked participants [@Petrenko2013DepIR; @Chen2025LocAgent], but the reached source still requires inspection and qualification.

The required-evidence audit nevertheless shows a contribution that file ranking alone understates. Full Workspace retains more required units than the graphless condition, and pandas 16499 demonstrates how owner resolution can preserve the identity of a relevant class even when the required pieces do not form a connected mechanism. In the graphless runs, the correct file and interior test methods survive, while the class declarations that expose the collection defect do not. CodeGraph is useful here because it restores an enclosing source owner, not because graph traversal discovers a long causal path. This supports a conditional claim: structural resolution can improve ownership and source survival in particular representations, but the evaluation does not establish a universal CodeGraph ranking or completeness benefit.

The connected-versus-independent comparison does not isolate a separate graph effect. Connected cases have lower joint-completeness rates in Full Workspace and Codex but slightly higher unit-survival rates. They also contain more required units and much more often span several files. Required-file count provides the clearer pattern: joint completeness falls sharply as the reference spans more files, and neither system completes the five- or six-file cases. The evidence therefore supports a multi-file assembly problem, but it does not show that connectedness itself causes the difficulty. Pandas 10068 illustrates the distinction: Full Workspace finds each required unit in at least one repetition, yet never retains all three together. The problem is not simply whether each part can be found, but whether the correct combination survives within one bounded final context.

RQ2 consequently has a qualified answer. Adaptive exploration modestly improves ranking and exact-source survival but incurs the dominant native model-token cost. CodeGraph contributes conditionally through structural ownership, exact navigation, and survival of particular source units, while its aggregate ranking effect remains mixed. Neither capability guarantees complete multi-file evidence, and their measured interaction is limited.

## RQ3: Controlled and Agentic Retrieval

The third research question compares Full Workspace with Codex as complete retrieval systems. Codex achieves stronger recall, NDCG, implementation-Oracle recovery, and required-evidence completeness. Its broader output is part of this advantage: Codex returns more files on average and represents more required files. The additional output is not merely noise because P@5 also favours Codex, although many selected files remain outside the implementation Oracle and 85 Codex runs still lack at least one complete required unit.

Full Workspace offers a different profile. It more often places an implementation file first and uses substantially fewer model tokens. Its explicit lifecycle also exposes intermediate evidence state and produces conservative completeness judgements. Codex completes sooner despite using approximately 3.6 times as many retrieval-flow tokens, showing that elapsed time and provider-reported token use capture different operational costs. The comparison therefore presents a trade-off among recall, prioritisation, resource use, and observability rather than a single ordering of the systems.

The systems' confidence outputs reinforce this conclusion. Codex marks every run `strong` and sufficient, while the independent required-evidence reference finds only 55 complete Codex runs. Full Workspace is far more conservative, but its low confidence is not itself proof that its internal assessment is better calibrated for every request. The external reference establishes only that universal Codex confidence is not equivalent to measured source completeness. Moreover, Codex exposes final source ranges without a comparable internal lifecycle trace. Its omissions cannot be assigned to search, navigation, context construction, or final selection using the same boundary analysis.

These differences cannot be attributed to agentic navigation alone. Workspace and Codex differ in their search interfaces, query construction, navigation policy, stopping behaviour, context size, consolidation, and final-selection process. RQ3 therefore supports a complete-system conclusion: in the evaluated configurations, Codex recovers broader and more complete evidence, whereas Workspace offers stronger first-rank precision, lower model-token use, and greater visibility into evidence construction.

## Implications for Repository Assistance and Learning

The findings suggest that repository assistance should expose evidence state rather than treating retrieval as a hidden prelude to generation. A file hit is useful, but it does not show that the responsible range, its caller, or its downstream effect survives. Systems can use explicit evidence requirements and honest partial outcomes to avoid presenting incomplete localisation as a complete causal account. Broader result sets may increase coverage, but they should not be interpreted as proof that the evidence is correctly prioritised or understood.

This matters for learning-oriented assistance because guided explanations and understanding checks can reinforce an unsupported account as easily as a supported one. Self-explanation and Socratic questioning motivate asking learners to articulate relationships [@Bisra2018SelfExplanation; @Tamang2021Socratic], but the present evaluation does not test whether Guided Intelligence improves learning. It establishes only the retrieval foundation from which such interaction is generated. User studies remain necessary to assess comprehension, trust, cognitive load, and the value of exposing uncertainty.

The same boundary applies to continued conversations. The evaluated front-end creates an independent state for each request, so evidence reuse, refresh, and invalidation across turns remain untested. A multi-turn system would need to decide when previously selected source remains applicable, when repository or conversational changes invalidate it, and when retrieval must run again.

## Validity and Generalisability

The required-evidence reference strengthens the evaluation beyond file overlap, but it introduces researcher judgement. Fixing artifacts identify the relevant responsibility, and exact pre-resolution paths, ranges, anchors, and hashes make the definitions reproducible. They do not make the minimum evidence set unique. A second review identifies six cases with judgment-sensitive scope, including absence, broad API, performance, formatting, and comment-maintenance cases. No independent annotator agreement is available. The reported completeness rates should therefore be interpreted as performance against this documented reference rather than a universal decomposition of each issue.

Boundary attribution has a related limitation. Final-selection omissions are explicit trace events, while earlier first-unavailable boundaries are inferred from successive observations. This supports stage-level localisation of evidence loss, not causal proof about the exact internal decision. Connectedness results are also descriptive because connection, number of required units, and number of files are confounded in the selected corpus.

Internal validity benefits from four repetitions and fixed repository snapshots, indexes, conditions, and model configuration. Model-backed stages remain stochastic, and hosted services prevent bit-for-bit reproduction. The native factorial comparison supports attribution to CodeGraph and the controller under the evaluated configuration; the Workspace–Codex comparison does not support component-level attribution. External validity is limited by 35 historical cases from TypeScript, pandas, and Vue, their language ecosystems, static-analysis boundaries, and repository-specific exclusions.

## Ethics and Responsible Use

The evaluation uses public open-source repositories and involves no recruited participants. Nevertheless, repository-assistance systems can expose licensed source to external providers, produce plausible but unsupported explanations, and encourage overreliance when their evidence is incomplete. Deployment with private repositories would require explicit data-governance, access-control, retention, and provider-policy decisions beyond this study.

The work also requires transparent use of generative AI during research and writing. Model output is treated as assistance rather than authority; the researcher remains responsible for verifying source, citations, analysis, and submitted prose. Within the artifact, provenance, bounded retrieval, and partial outcomes reduce but do not eliminate the risk of unsupported claims. These safeguards are especially important in learning settings, where a confident but incorrect explanation may shape a learner's mental model.

## Future Work

The first priority is independent validation. Additional annotators could assess the required-evidence reference, while response-level evaluation could test whether a model correctly interprets relationships between jointly available snippets. Second, the distributed lifecycle trace could be materialised as one lineage attached to each final evidence item, reducing the need to infer early boundaries across events. Third, stronger source-span preservation and final selection should target the observed multi-file assembly problem.

Further work should extend structural recovery beyond static relationships, reduce the cost of adaptive exploration, and evaluate evidence reuse across conversation turns. Finally, user studies are required before claiming that evidence-grounded explanations or understanding checks improve learning. The central implication remains narrower: inspectable evidence construction strengthens what can be evaluated and qualified, but it does not itself guarantee understanding.
