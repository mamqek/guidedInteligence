# Conclusion

This thesis investigates whether repository retrieval can find and retain the specific source evidence needed to explain software behaviour within a bounded context. Guided Intelligence provides the experimental setting: its native Workspace mode separates retrieval, structural resolution, semantic qualification, bounded adaptive exploration, and final evidence selection. The evaluation compares four Workspace configurations and Codex across 35 CodeRepoQA-derived testcases and 700 accepted runs. Alongside file-ranking measures, a corpus-wide reference tracks the joint survival of 78 exact source units from the pre-resolution repositories.

The resulting architecture makes evidence construction inspectable. Source observations retain stable identities and records of how they are discovered, qualified, and selected. Navigation leads remain distinct from evidence that supports a claim, and final selection is recorded as its own decision. These boundaries make it possible to determine whether required source remains available and to locate the first stage at which it becomes unavailable. They do not guarantee that every semantic decision is correct, but they expose decisions that would otherwise remain hidden within a single retrieval result.

The component results show that additional retrieval machinery is useful only when its contribution remains controlled. Adaptive exploration produces modest improvements in file ranking and required-source survival, but accounts for most of Workspace's additional model-token cost. CodeGraph slightly improves recall and required-unit survival without consistently improving the earliest ranks. Its value lies in particular cases where enclosing-owner identity and exact structural navigation preserve source that flat ranges represent poorly. These conditional benefits justify retaining both capabilities, while the incomplete multi-file results rule out treating either as a general solution to evidence assembly.

The complete-system comparison reveals a broader trade-off. Codex recovers more relevant files, returns broader evidence sets, and contains every required unit in 55 of 140 runs, compared with 23 for Full Workspace. Full Workspace more often ranks an implementation file first, uses substantially fewer model tokens, and exposes how evidence moves through its retrieval stages. Neither system reliably assembles large evidence sets: Full Workspace completes no run whose reference spans three or more files, and Codex completes no five- or six-file case. The comparison therefore favours neither system unconditionally. Codex provides broader recovery, whereas Workspace provides stronger prioritisation, lower cost, and greater observability under the evaluated configurations.

The thesis contributes the Guided Intelligence artifact, a controlled repository-evidence lifecycle, bounded adaptive exploration, and an evaluation that moves beyond file overlap to exact-source survival. That evaluation shows why finding a relevant file is not enough. The responsible source range may still be absent, different required units may appear across separate runs without surviving together, and evidence found early may disappear before final selection. A system's own confidence is also insufficient: Codex labels every evaluated result strong and sufficient while remaining incomplete against the external required-evidence reference in 85 runs.

These conclusions apply to the evaluated repositories, configurations, and researcher-constructed evidence reference. Earlier evidence-loss boundaries are inferred from successive stage observations, Codex provides no comparable internal trace, and the study does not evaluate the correctness of generated explanations or their effect on learning. Future work should focus on improving multi-file evidence assembly and validating whether the retained evidence supports correct explanations.

The central conclusion is that repository assistance should not equate plausible file retrieval with understanding. A stronger system must retain the source needed to support its explanation, show how that source becomes evidence, and state when important evidence remains unresolved. Inspectability cannot guarantee a correct explanation, but it provides a firmer basis for producing, evaluating, and trusting one.
